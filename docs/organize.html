How to organize papers

When every paper in a workspace sits at the same level, people and AI lose track of where to put things and where to look once there are a few hundred. This compares whether to have a hierarchy, and if so what shape.

Drafted 2026-11-21. The same day the container changed from "folders" to "nested papers" (a folder is the same as "a summary paper plus a list of children", so it is redundant). Continues Model and concepts; decision numbers continue from D19. Decisions are carried into Model and concepts.

Note (11-21): D15 in the model reduced commits and releases to "versions" alone (a copy of a paper saved on purpose by a person or an AI; the system never makes one). There is no autosave; otherwise there is only the current state. So D22 (recording moves as commits) and D32 (MoveID) here are withdrawn, the "release" of D26 is a version, /latest and /v/{n} in D29 point to versions, and D30 (anyone may save, AI included) stands. Everything decided is in Model and concepts, which is the source of truth; this page records how the decisions were reached.

What goes wrong when everything is flat

Finding things and getting an overview are covered by search, sorted lists and an outline for AI. What a flat space really lacks is handling things together, which shows up here.

SituationWhen flat
Narrowing search and lists"Search within this project" is impossible; only the whole workspace can be searched.
What AI should read"Write this taking all the project's material into account" leaves the AI guessing which papers that is. MCP reads and searches cannot be given a scope.
Cleaning upA finished project's material cannot be archived, deleted or moved to another workspace together; it is one by one.
ExportingA project's material cannot be exported as one set to Markdown or PDF.
WatchingThere is no unit for "tell me when something in this project changes" (once there are notifications).

Also, a summary of a group is hard to define: there is no place for writing about a group as a whole, such as the project's overview, an index of its material and its current conclusions. A "summary paper" in a flat space does not show structurally what it summarizes (when nested, the parent's body is the summary).

And canonical and throwaway papers mix. AI produces many meeting notes, interim findings and drafts, and organized knowledge gets buried among them. That is a matter of how settled a paper is, not of where it is, and is better expressed by a state than by the hierarchy (D26).

Not a problem (considered)

  • Material for only some people: expressed by workspaces. Groups do not carry access scopes.
  • Writing rules per scope: not essential; the workspace's description is enough.
  • Order within a group: not essential.
  • Referring by path: URLs are enough.
  • Clashing names: showing the creator and the updated time in search results is enough.

So what is needed is "naming a set of papers" and "having a summary of that set", which a single level of groups could satisfy for the situations above. But organizing needs nesting, because that is the only shape people can grasp: nobody takes in hundreds of items at once; people split them into groups of a few to a dozen and group those again. AI handles flat sets with search and outlines, but for people to see the whole and know where things are, nesting is needed (a project split into research, procedures and retrospectives, say). So papers nest (D19), with no depth limit (D20). Belonging to exactly one group (not several, as with tags) makes it clear what a cleanup or an export includes.

Splitting into finer workspaces could stand in for this, but workspaces are defined in configuration files (an administrator's job), so they cannot serve everyday organizing.

Candidate shapes

A. Tags

No hierarchy. Papers get several tags and are filtered by them.

B. Folders

A container separate from papers. Folders nest, and a paper is in exactly one folder.

C. Nested papers

A paper has a parent. The list of children shows outside the body (below it, or in the sidebar).

F. Child papers embedded as blocks

The Notion way. A child paper is a "paper" block in the parent's body, placed where it was written.

D. Index papers and links

No structure; an "index" paper listing links to papers ties them together (the wiki way).

What a move does

This section uses these names.

  • P: the paper being moved (with n papers under it)
  • O: P's old parent (O's body holds P's paper block)
  • N: P's new parent (P's paper block goes into N's body)
  • X: the block in N's body right before where P goes

Example: move "DB switchover" (P) under "Steps" (O) to after the "Overview" paragraph (X) in the body of "Infrastructure" (N).

What is written

TargetChangeSame transaction?
O's bodyP's paper block is deleted (one more block version). A commit on Oyes (three in one)
N's bodyP's paper block is inserted after X as a new block (blocks never cross papers, so O's block is not moved). A commit on N
PParentID changes. A move_paper commit on P. The three commits are tied by one move ID (D32)
The n papers under PNothing is written. They only point to their parent (P), so they follow P—
Search entriesUnchanged. Papers keep no ancestors, so a move rewrites nothing. "Search under this paper" walks up from each hit (hits are few, so the walk is short)—

Checks before accepting

  • P is not the home. P is public (private papers are not in the tree; making one public is a move "from nowhere to N").
  • N is a public paper of the same workspace, not in the trash. Moving across workspaces is a separate copy-and-delete (D35).
  • No cycle: N is neither P nor under P (walking up from N never reaches P).
  • The depth after the move stays within the safety valve (100). The depth under P is not counted; the valve works as "stop walking after 100 levels".
  • N may be archived. Archiving is a mark on each paper and is not inherited (P is not archived).

When things happen at the same time

Concurrent eventsResult
Two people move the same P to different placesThe last wins. The transaction reads and writes P, so the later one moves it again from the earlier result (the new O).
A moves X under Y while B moves Y under X (a cycle together)Each transaction reads the other's paper, so one is retried, finds the cycle and is refused.
During P's move, someone deletes P's paper block in O's body (= trash with its subtree)Whichever finishes first wins. If the move is first, the deletion finds nothing; if the deletion is first, the move is refused because P is in the trash.
During P's move, an ancestor of N movesP's transaction reads the parents from N up to the root, so a change in any of them retries it with the new chain.
X is deleted or moved at the same time in N's bodyThe rule of any insert (after the closest live block before X, else at the top).
O's or N's body is edited at the same timeAdding or removing a paper block conflicts with no other block's contents; order is last-writer-wins.

History and releases

  • The move appears in the histories of O, N and P, which point to each other through the move's ID: who moved what from where to where.
  • O's older releases (v2, say) contain P's paper block. Opening v2 shows P as "now under N", linking to today's P.
  • Restoring O to an older version does not bring P's paper block back: P left O's care when it moved (D34).
  • P's own releases do not change (the parent is not part of a release). The breadcrumbs of P's /v/{n} show where it is now.

Derived data and screens

  • Breadcrumbs and paths change. Paths are not stored, so nothing is rewritten, but the "place" people remembered changes. URLs and links use IDs and do not break.
  • Pins are IDs and stay. If P lands under another pinned paper, it shows twice in the sidebar (the same paper).
  • Pages showing O, N, P or what is under them get the change through the feed, and their sidebars and breadcrumbs update.
  • The scope of "search, archive or export under this paper" changes (P leaves O's subtree and joins N's); so would a future "watch this subtree".
  • Visibility and access do not change (same workspace, still public).
  • There is no special undo; undoing is "move it back".

Recommended: child papers embedded as blocks (F)

No container concept. A child paper exists as a "paper" block in its parent's body, so the parent's body is where the children are introduced and summarized.

On screen

  • Creating a child: "Add a paper" anywhere in the text. A paper block appears there and the new child opens.
  • Moving a child: dragging its paper block within the parent's body reorders it; another parent is chosen with "Move" (a path works too).
  • Opening a paper: the path (breadcrumbs) and state (v3, canonical, …) at the top, then the text.
  • The whole tree is never shown. The sidebar shows only the papers the user pinned and what is under them (D33). The ways in are the workspace's home (D31), pins, search and breadcrumbs.

Added block type

paper
A child paper's block. Its only property is the child's ID; the title and state shown are the child's. It sits only at the top level of a body (not in table cells).

Added to papers

ParentID
The parent paper. Always matches the paper block in the parent's body (written in the same transaction). Empty for the home and for private papers.
ReleaseNumber
The latest release. 0 is a draft, 1 or more canonical (D26).
ArchivedAt
When it was archived; set means archived.

The order of children is the order of blocks in the parent's body (not stored separately).

Rules

  • Every public paper except the home sits in exactly one parent's body as exactly one paper block.
  • Children are created and moved by whoever may use the parent (and the paper being moved), and AI acting for them. No administrator rights are needed.
  • No depth limit (D20).
  • Moves are last-writer-wins. Papers cannot move across workspaces.
  • A parent's release contains paper blocks (child IDs) but not the children's contents; each child has its own releases.

Reading

Nothing depends on the total number of papers.

  • The children are known from the parent's body; their titles and states come from one batch read of the child IDs in the paper blocks.
  • Opening a branch in the sidebar reads only its children, with the (ParentID, …) index.
  • Breadcrumbs walk up one parent at a time (as many reads as the depth). The server keeps a "paper → parent" map, updated from the change feed, so usually no reads are needed (D28).
  • "Search under this paper": walk up from each hit and keep those under the target.
  • "Archive or export everything under this paper": descend from the target one level at a time with the children index. It is rare, so it runs in the background.

Showing and naming by path

  • Showing: ancestors' titles joined as security / Steps / DB switchover. Paths are not stored, so renaming a paper on the way rewrites nothing under it.
  • Naming (AI, search): AI may give a parent by path (parent_path: "Steps/DB switchover"). If a parent has several children with the same title, the candidates come back and the AI picks one by ID.
  • URLs stay IDs (/ws/{ws}/p/{id}).

How AI uses it

  • papers_guide returns the home's text. The AI reads the summary and follows paper blocks down the branches it needs.
  • Creating a child is one commit op (insert of type paper, with the child's title); after decides where in the parent's body it goes.
  • Reads and searches can be scoped to "under this paper".

Decisions

The problems F brings back (place in versions, deleting blocks and children, moves) are solved in Concerns with embedding. E's readability comes from building paths on the fly.

D20. Depth (decided)

No limit. The implementation stops at 100 levels as a safety valve against broken data or a runaway AI; it is not a limit of the specification. Deep breadcrumbs elide the middle.

D21. Deleting a paper with children (decided: to the trash with its children)

Deleting a paper with children sends everything under it to the trash, and restoring brings it all back. Assumes the model's D4-A (papers go to a trash and are deleted for good after 30 days).

Note: deleting (to the trash, restoring, purging) is the creator's alone, and a subtree only when everything in it is theirs. Others archive (D18 in the model).

  • Remembering what went together: each paper under it gets DeletedAt and DeletedWith (the ID of the paper whose deletion started it). Restoring brings back only those with the same DeletedWith; children deleted on their own earlier stay.
  • Large subtrees: written in parts when one transaction is not enough. Marks go on the subtree first and on the starting paper last. If it stops midway, repeating the same deletion finishes it (it only sets the same marks, so repeating is harmless).
  • Restoring only a child: a child whose parent is still in the trash goes under the nearest ancestor that is not (or the top).
  • Visibility: lists, search and reads leave out papers with DeletedAt. Search entries are kept and filtered after searching (so large subtrees do not rewrite all their search entries).
  • Purging: after 30 days, everything with the same DeletedWith is deleted down to blocks, versions, commits and search entries.

D22. Recording paper moves in history (decided)

  • A move is a commit on the moved paper: op move_paper, change "old parent → new parent". Who moved it (and for whom, for AI) is its writer, as with other commits.
  • Nothing is written to the papers under it, so they get no commits; their histories show the move through the commit on the paper it started from.
  • Likewise, deleting and restoring a subtree (D21) is a commit on the starting paper.
  • A position is not contents, so moves are never conflicts (last writer wins).

D23 (who may create folders), D24 (whether papers can sit at the top) and D25 (folder summaries) were withdrawn with folders. Whoever may use the parent creates children, papers can sit at the top, and the summary is the parent's body.

D26. Separating canonical from throwaway (direction decided: releases stack versions)

A canonical state needs proper versioning. Becoming canonical is v1, and afterwards versions stack as v2, v3 … when users say so. This is separate from block versions (bumped automatically on every edit), so to avoid confusion it is called a release (shown as v1, v2 …).

States

Canonical never goes back to draft (releases are not withdrawn). What is no longer needed is archived; archiving also tidies up subtrees.

What it allows

  • The difference between any two releases (comparing block versions), and the changes since the last release.
  • Setting a paper back to a release: a commit restoring each block to that version (history is not rewritten).
  • URLs pointing to a release (…/p/{ID}/v/2, D29), stable as references.

There is no /edit (the plain URL does that). Use the plain URL to write together and see the present, /latest to point to settled contents, and /v/{n} to point to exact wording.

  • /latest of a draft (no release): says "No release yet" and leads to the plain URL.
  • The plain URL: with releases, shows "n changes since v3 (n by AI)" and links to the diff and /latest; this also nudges writers to release.
  • Links in text, paper blocks, the sidebar and search results: to the plain URL. A link can also be written to /latest or /v/{n}.
  • Search: in the current state.
  • AI reads (MCP): the current state by default; at (current / latest / a number) chooses. An AI cites /v/{n} as the source of an answer (it never changes later).

D30. Who may release (decided: anyone)

A release fixes a paper's contents at that moment (title and all blocks) as v1, v2 … when a user says "stack a version here". The first release (v1) turns the paper from a draft into a canonical paper (D26). It has its own name because it differs from block versions, which go up automatically on every edit.

Decided: anyone who may use the paper may release it (there are no roles, so as everywhere else).

  • A release records its writer (person or AI, and for whom), shown in the list of versions and on the plain URL ("v3 · Alice", "v4 · Claude (for Alice)").
  • Like commits, releases are never withdrawn. If one is unwanted, set the paper back to the previous release and release again.

D27. Private papers sit flat outside the tree (decided)

Like Notion's "Private" section, private papers stay out of the shared tree, in a flat list of one's own within the workspace.

  • A private paper has neither parent nor children (ParentID is empty). Only public papers are in the shared tree.
  • Making a paper public is placing it somewhere in the tree: choose a parent (or the top). One commit records both the visibility change and the move.
  • Only a public paper without children can become private; it leaves the tree for one's own list. A paper with children has its children moved first.
  • So the tree never has an "invisible parent" or "invisible child", and deleting a subtree (D21) never involves someone else's private paper.

D28. Reading the tree (decided: only the parent is kept)

Moves may carry thousands or tens of thousands of papers (re-hanging a large branch near the top).

  • A. Each paper keeps its ancestors' IDs: breadcrumbs and subtree scopes take one read, but moving a branch rewrites every paper under it: minutes of background work for tens of thousands of papers, during which moves and deletions under it must stop and breadcrumbs are off.
  • B. Only the parent is kept: a move writes three documents (old parent, new parent, the paper) and is instant however large the subtree. Breadcrumbs walk up as many parents as the depth, and subtree scopes descend one level at a time.
Decided: B. Earlier, moves were only assumed to be rare and their size was not estimated. If tens of thousands of papers move, A brings a large background job with every move and the handling of inconsistencies midway (stopping, resuming). With B a move is always three writes, in line with the lock-free design. B's read costs are kept down like this.
  • Breadcrumbs and cycle checks: the server keeps a "paper → parent" map updated from the change feed. The parent is the truth, so transactions read it again to confirm.
  • "Search under this paper": walk up from the hits; hits are the top few dozen, so the walk is small.
  • Handling a whole subtree (archive, export): rare, so a background job that descends one level at a time with the children index.

D31. Every workspace has a home paper (decided)

Each workspace has one root paper, the home. Top-level papers are embedded in the home's body. One rule then describes the whole tree: "every public paper except the home is in exactly one parent's body".

  • Creation: workspaces are defined in configuration, so at startup each configured workspace gets a home if it has none (exactly one however often the server starts; "create if missing" in a transaction). Its first title is the configured name, with an empty body.
  • Which is the home: storage keeps a workspace record (workspaces/{ws}) with HomePaperID. The home's ID has the same format as any paper's.
  • What it allows: editing its text and title, releasing, and placing children with paper blocks, like any paper.
  • What it does not allow: deletion (including the trash), moves, and becoming private. Deleting a paper block in the home deletes the child (to the trash with its subtree); the home stays.
  • Division of roles: the configured description holds "rules for AI" (changed by deploying). The home's text is "the workspace's summary and entrance, for people and AI" (anyone may write it).
  • Removing a workspace from the configuration keeps its data, home included (D13 in the model).

D32. A move makes a commit on each of the three papers, tied by the move's ID (decided)

A move spans the old parent (block removed), the new parent (block added) and the paper itself (parent changed). Keeping the model in which commits belong to papers, each paper gets a commit.

  • The three commits share a MoveID, so each leads to the others. Writer, time and message are the same.
  • They are written in one transaction, so none survives alone.
  • The same applies to making a private paper public (placing it under a parent) and a public one private (taking it out). Those are two (the parent and the paper).

D33. The sidebar shows pins with what is under them, and recent papers (decided)

The whole tree is not shown.

  • Pins: papers the user chose, per user and workspace, in the user's own order. The model gets Pin (user, workspace, paper, order key). Nobody else is affected (no roles needed).
  • Under a pin: only the open levels are read, with the children index.
  • Recent: the few papers opened most recently (say 10), shown automatically. Stored so that it is the same on every device (one record per user and workspace, updated on every open).
  • Entrance: the home (D31) is always shown; it is a newcomer's first way in.
  • A pinned paper in the trash is not shown (it is again once restored). Pins are IDs, so they survive moves. One's own private papers can be pinned.

D34. Restoring an old version does not bring back children that moved elsewhere (decided)

Example: on October 1 the body of "Steps" held the paper block of its child "DB switchover". Later someone moved "DB switchover" under "Infrastructure" (the block left "Steps" and was added to "Infrastructure"). Restoring "Steps" to October 1 would bring the block back too, but "DB switchover" is now under "Infrastructure": restored as it is, one paper would sit in two places.

A child that moved left its old parent's care when it moved. Restoring "Steps" sets the contents of "Steps" itself to October 1; it does not change where other papers are.

  • The text and title of "Steps" go back to October 1, except the paper blocks of children that moved elsewhere (nothing is put in their place).
  • No confirmation; the result says "1 child that moved elsewhere ("DB switchover", now under "Infrastructure") was not restored". Restoring through AI or the API returns the same.
  • Children in the trash are different: a child trashed by deleting its block in the body of "Steps" is still in its care. If restoring brings the block back, the child comes back from the trash too (D21), unless it was purged after 30 days.
  • Viewing an old version (/v/{n} or history) shows the children as they were, noting "now under Infrastructure" with a link to where they are.

D35. Moving across workspaces is "copy, then delete" (decided)

Screens and the API call it a move, but it is a copy into the target workspace followed by deleting the original with its subtree (D21). Workspaces have separate storage paths, so papers do not keep their IDs.

Flow

  1. Check that the person may use the target workspace and that the paper chosen as parent there can take it (public, not in the trash).
  2. Copy P and its subtree with new IDs into the target (a background job; a large subtree takes time, so progress is shown). While copying, the originals can be read but not written.
  3. When the copy is done, the original goes to the trash with its subtree (restorable for 30 days).
  4. A forward from each old ID to its new place is kept.

What is copied

WhatHow
Title and text (current state)Copied.
The latest releaseIf the original has one, its contents become the copy's v1, and the difference between the current state and that release is kept. An original without releases stays a draft.
Older releases, block versions, commitsNot copied. The copy's first commit records "moved from {workspace}/{ID} by …". The original's history can be seen while it is in the trash (30 days).
Paper blocks and links within the subtreeThose pointing inside the subtree are switched to the new IDs; links pointing outside stay (they keep pointing to papers in the old workspace).
privatePrivate papers are copied into one's own private list in the target.
Search entriesMade again in the target; the old ones go with the deletion.

After the move

  • Old URLs: forwarded to the new place through the forward. People who may not use the target workspace still get "not found" (404), so the target does not leak. Forwards are never deleted (they are small).
  • Pins: a pin on an old ID is switched to the new ID through the forward.
  • Who can see it changes: the target workspace's rule decides. The move's confirmation says "who can see it will change".

D36 (moving large subtrees) was withdrawn: with B for D28 (only the parent kept), nothing under a moved paper is rewritten.
